Draft 4 builds on draft 2. It keeps the six course learning outcomes (CLOs),
with the IDE and the AI assistant kept out of them, and adds what draft 2
lacked: PRIMM as the way every week works, a gentler way into programming, and
a clear line from each week's goal to its module learning outcomes (MLOs) and
CLOs.

This is the Staged weeks version. The MLOs are rewritten so that each module's
four climb PRIMM: x.1 predict and run, x.2 investigate, x.3 modify, x.4 make. A
week is one arc.

Drafts 3, 4, and 5 are three versions of the same idea, to choose between or
borrow from. They share the CLOs, the toolkit, and the weekly hooks, and differ
in how PRIMM is arranged; the comparison near the end sets them side by side.
Each of the 10 weekly modules has four MLOs, 40 in all, and every MLO names the
earlier MLOs it builds on.

Worked examples for every module are on the draft 4 examples page, linked under
each module below. Assessments come later, once the outcomes are agreed.

.. rubric:: Reading the tables

- **Builds on** lists the earlier MLOs a student needs first. "Prior math"
  means the algebra students bring from school.

- **CLO** lists the course learning outcomes the MLO serves. Each CLO is
  explained in "MLOs by course outcome" below.

- **PRIMM step** is the stage the MLO is: predict and run, investigate, modify,
  or make.

- **AI assistant** means the one built into the IDE: JetBrains AI Assistant in
  PyCharm, or GitHub Copilot in VS Code.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The course learning outcomes.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Six CLOs, unchanged from draft 2. Each maps to PLO-1 and PLO-2, as in the
proposal.

.. list-table::
    :header-rows: 1
    :widths: 8 12 60 20

    * - CLO
      - Name
      - Outcome
      - PLO
    * - CLO-1
      - Basics
      - Write and run Python programs that compute with variables, expressions,
        and built-in types, take input, and produce exact output.
      - PLO-1, PLO-2
    * - CLO-2
      - Control
      - Write programs that make decisions and repeat actions using Boolean
        logic, selection, and loops.
      - PLO-1, PLO-2
    * - CLO-3
      - Functions
      - Write functions that break a problem into reusable parts, and organize
        code for reuse in modules and simple classes.
      - PLO-1, PLO-2
    * - CLO-4
      - Data
      - Store, retrieve, and process data using lists, dictionaries, tuples,
        sets, and text files, and choose the structure that fits the task.
      - PLO-1, PLO-2
    * - CLO-5
      - Evaluate
      - Evaluate programs by predicting and tracing how they run, testing them
        with typical and boundary cases, debugging defects, and comparing
        alternative implementations.
      - PLO-1, PLO-2
    * - CLO-6
      - Solve
      - Solve an unfamiliar problem by combining functions, data structures,
        and files, and make the solution handle errors and bad input safely.
      - PLO-1, PLO-2

.. rubric:: Why these six

- They follow the catalog description: data types and expressions (CLO-1),
  conditions and loops (CLO-2), procedures and modularity (CLO-3), and methods
  of program design (CLO-6), plus data (CLO-4) and evaluating programs (CLO-5).

- Each CLO names one kind of thing a student can do by the end of the course,
  so each can be assessed and reported on its own.

- Tools and AI are not CLOs. Students use PyCharm and an AI assistant all term,
  but they are the means, not the outcome. The MLOs that involve them serve the
  CLOs they help with.

- The old CLO-2 named three topics and left out functions, selection, strings,
  and files, so it is split into CLO-2 (control), CLO-3 (functions and
  classes), and CLO-4 (data).

- Python compiles a file to bytecode by itself, so "compile" isn't something
  students do. CLO-1 says write and run instead, and the compile-then-run idea
  is something students explain, under CLO-5.

.. _css225-draft-4-primm:

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PRIMM in this course.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every week follows PRIMM (Sentance, Waite and Kallia, 2019): students Predict
what code will do, Run it to check, Investigate why it behaved that way, Modify
it in small steps, and finally Make something of their own. Reading and tracing
come before writing, so students are never asked to produce code they haven't
first understood. The MLO says what students can do; the PRIMM stages are how
they get there.

.. list-table::
    :header-rows: 1
    :widths: 12 36 24 28

    * - Stage
      - What students do
      - What it builds
      - How it eases in
    * - Predict
      - Write down what given code will do before running it, alone and then
        with a partner.
      - Predicting and tracing.
      - The code is short, and already works.
    * - Run
      - Run it and compare with the prediction.
      - Checking, and reading output and errors.
      - A wrong prediction is expected, and never graded.
    * - Investigate
      - Explain why it behaved that way: trace it, annotate it, experiment with
        it.
      - Explaining and comparing.
      - Guiding questions are given.
    * - Modify
      - Change the working code in small steps, predicting each change.
      - Fixing and extending.
      - One change at a time.
    * - Make
      - Write something new from the same ideas.
      - Writing, building, and solving.
      - Help fades from Complete it to Design it.

In this version the PRIMM stages are the MLOs. A module's four MLOs are x.1
predict and run, x.2 investigate, x.3 modify, and x.4 make, so a week is one
PRIMM arc and the MLO table itself shows the climb from reading to writing. The
MLOs are rewritten to fit.

.. _css225-draft-4-ease:

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Easing in.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The target is the same for everyone. What changes is how gently students get
there.

- **One new idea at a time.** Each MLO is met on its own, with a hook that
  raises a question rather than adding content.

- **Read before you write.** Students predict, run, and investigate code that
  already works before they change it, and change it before they write their
  own. The first thing they write is half-finished.

- **Help fades on a ramp.** The Make step starts as Complete it in Weeks 1 and
  2 and ends as Design it in Week 10, so nobody meets a blank page before they
  can use it.

- **Mistakes are data.** Students keep a prediction log for the first four
  weeks, a wrong prediction is never marked down, and each week names an error
  to meet on purpose.

- **Setup comes before Week 1.** A setup checklist (Python 3.12 or newer,
  PyCharm, the ``css225`` project, AI code completion off) goes out a week
  early, and the first 15 minutes of Week 1 help anyone who hasn't finished it.

- **Core and stretch.** Every cycle has one stretch task for students who have
  programmed before. The core is all an MLO needs.

.. rubric:: The Make ramp

Help with the Make step fades over the term, in four levels:

.. list-table::
    :header-rows: 1
    :widths: 20 80

    * - Level
      - What it means
    * - Complete it
      - A nearly finished program with a few gaps, or shuffled lines to put in
        order.
    * - Extend it
      - A working starter file, with comments saying where each new piece goes.
    * - Build it
      - A plain-language spec and checks to pass; you write the code.
    * - Design it
      - The problem only; you choose the structure and write your own checks.

.. list-table::
    :header-rows: 1
    :widths: 8 16 44 32

    * - Week
      - Make
      - What is given
      - Students decide
    * - 1
      - Complete it
      - The variable names and the print lines. Students fill in the two
        conversion expressions.
      - The conversions.
    * - 2
      - Complete it
      - A badge template with the frame drawn and gaps for the values.
      - What the badge says.
    * - 3
      - Extend it
      - The toolkit functions and an outline of the questions and the summary.
      - How the functions fit together.
    * - 4
      - Extend it
      - A skeleton with a docstring and one ``assert`` as a model.
      - The rule and the conditions.
    * - 5
      - Extend it
      - A starter file with ``import random`` and a comment marking each
        missing piece.
      - The loop and when it stops.
    * - 6
      - Extend it
      - The data, and the shape of the report.
      - The loops and the lookups.
    * - 7
      - Build it
      - A plain-language spec and checks for each command.
      - The code.
    * - 8
      - Build it
      - Last week's to-do list and its checks.
      - Which standard-library tools to use.
    * - 9
      - Build it
      - A spec and checks for each helper, including the bad-input cases.
      - How and where to handle each error.
    * - 10
      - Design it
      - The problem statement only.
      - The structure, the checks, and which suggestions to keep.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AI across the term.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The AI assistant comes in step by step, and it is used inside the outcomes it
helps with rather than being an outcome itself. Every MLO that involves it asks
students to check what it says: the assistant suggests, and the trace and the
tests decide. 4.2, 7.2, 8.2, 9.2, and 10.4 serve CLO-5, and 9.2 and 10.4 also
serve CLO-6.

.. list-table::
    :header-rows: 1
    :widths: 10 10 80

    * - Week
      - MLO
      - What students do with the AI assistant
    * - 1-3
      - 1.1
      - Nothing: AI completion is turned off at setup, so students type every
        line themselves.
    * - 4
      - 4.2
      - Ask it to explain a failing test, then check the explanation by tracing
        the code.
    * - 7
      - 7.2
      - Ask it for test cases, and keep only the ones that add something.
    * - 8
      - 8.2
      - Test and fix code an AI wrote.
    * - 9
      - 9.2
      - Ask it for fixes, and accept them only when the tests pass.
    * - 10
      - 10.4
      - Judge its suggestions while solving an unfamiliar problem.

In person, the assistant is used only in instructor-led moments: the instructor
asks it on the projector and the class judges the answer. Online students may
use it freely from Week 4, and the same MLOs ask them to check its answers.
Weeks 5 and 6 have no AI MLO.

.. _css225-draft-4-compare:

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How the three versions compare.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Drafts 3, 4, and 5 share the CLOs, the weekly hooks, the toolkit, the setup
checklist, and the Make ramp. They differ in where PRIMM sits.

.. list-table::
    :header-rows: 1
    :widths: 14 24 24 20 18

    * - Version
      - PRIMM sits in
      - A week looks like
      - Strength
      - Watch for
    * - Draft 3: Cycles
      - One PRIMM cycle per MLO. The MLOs are draft 2's, unchanged.
      - Four cycles of about 25 minutes, then a Make that combines them.
      - The closest to draft 2, so the safest to adopt. Students meet one idea
        at a time.
      - Four ideas in one session is a quick pace for novices.
    * - Draft 4: Staged weeks
      - PRIMM is in the MLOs themselves: x.1 predict and run, x.2 investigate,
        x.3 modify, x.4 make.
      - One arc: predict and run the week's ideas, investigate them, modify
        them, then make.
      - The clearest alignment. The MLO table shows the climb, and every week
        has the same shape.
      - Students read for the first hour of a session before they write, and
        the MLOs are less specific.
    * - Draft 5: Meet then own
      - Students meet an idea one week and own it the next. The MLOs are draft
        2's, unchanged.
      - Own last week's ideas (modify, make), then meet this week's (predict,
        run, investigate).
      - The strongest on pacing: a week of settling sits between reading and
        writing.
      - Depends on students attending week after week, and Week 10 needs care.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Provisional choices to confirm or overturn.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. The proposal form showed "Reached max" after four CLOs. Check that it
   accepts six before relying on this set.

2. Neither the IDE nor AI literacy is a CLO. The proposal's rationale promises
   AI literacy, and the catalog description says students "explore integrated
   development environments (IDE)". Both now show up only in MLOs, so a
   course-level report can't show them. The description ("write and compile
   code") and the major topics list also need rewording to match.

3. The setup checklist before Week 1 asks for about 30 minutes before the term.
   If that isn't allowed, the first 15 minutes of Week 1 have to carry setup
   alone.

4. CLO-5 carries 25 of 40 MLOs, because the predict and investigate steps are
   evaluating code. That is the cost of putting PRIMM in the MLOs. It stays in
   every module, and the other five CLOs keep their spread.

5. The staged MLOs are less specific than draft 2's, and 8.2 joins two ideas
   (built-ins and AI-written code) because both are code you didn't write.

6. Still open from before: where the final exam goes (10.4's kind of problem
   could be its model), and which AI assistant licenses NLU covers.
